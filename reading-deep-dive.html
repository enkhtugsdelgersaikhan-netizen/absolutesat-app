Central Ideas and Details

Information & Ideas
What it tests

These questions test whether you can separate the main point of a short passage from supporting details. A central-idea question asks what the text is primarily doing or arguing; a detail question asks what the text explicitly states about a narrower point. The SAT rewards answers that are broad enough to cover the whole relevant passage but not so broad that they introduce claims the author never makes.

How the SAT disguises it
  • The passage contains several interesting details, but only one idea organizes them.
  • A choice repeats exact words from the passage yet describes only one example rather than the main point.
  • A main-idea choice adds a cause, judgment, or prediction not stated in the text.
  • A detail question paraphrases rather than repeats the sentence containing the answer.
Example problem

Marine biologist Lena Ortiz studied reef fish that shelter inside branching corals. During unusually warm months, some corals lost much of their branching structure even when they did not die. Ortiz found that fish abundance declined most sharply on reefs where structural complexity fell the most. She argues that heat can therefore affect reef communities not only by killing corals but also by changing the physical shelter those corals provide. Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  • A) Reef fish generally prefer warm water to cool water.
  • B) Coral death is the only mechanism by which heat affects reef fish.
  • C) Heat can alter reef fish communities by reducing the shelter provided by coral structure.
  • D) Branching corals recover from heat more quickly than other coral types.
Answer: C
Deep-dive solution
  1. Identify the passage's repeated relationship: heat → loss of coral structure → less shelter → fewer fish.
  2. Notice that the last sentence explicitly broadens the implication: heat matters even without coral death.
  3. Choice C captures that full relationship without adding anything new.
  4. Choice B directly contradicts the text. Choices A and D are not supported at all.
Fast method

After reading, finish the sentence 'The author mainly wants me to understand that…' in your own words before looking at choices.

Common trap

A true detail is not automatically the central idea. The best main idea must account for the passage as a whole.

Inferences

Information & Ideas
What it tests

Inference questions ask for the conclusion most strongly supported by what the passage says, even though that conclusion is not stated word-for-word. The correct answer must be a necessary or highly reasonable extension of the evidence—not merely something that could be true. Strong inference work is about staying conservative: choose the answer that requires the fewest assumptions.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A passage describes a pattern but never directly states its implication.
  • The answer choices differ mainly in strength: may, likely, always, never.
  • A tempting option is plausible in the real world but not supported by this passage.
  • The blank to complete appears after evidence, so the missing sentence must logically follow from that evidence.
Example problem

A historian examined shipping records from a nineteenth-century port. Before a new railway opened, imported grain typically remained in warehouses for six to eight days before being transported inland. In the two years after the railway opened, the average storage time fell to less than three days, while the total amount of grain entering the port changed little. Which conclusion is best supported by the text?

  • A) The railway probably made inland transport of grain faster or easier.
  • B) The railway caused merchants to import much more grain than before.
  • C) Grain quality declined after the railway opened.
  • D) Warehouse owners intentionally delayed grain shipments before the railway opened.
Answer: A
Deep-dive solution
  1. Focus on what changed: storage time dropped sharply.
  2. Focus on what did not change: total grain entering the port stayed about the same.
  3. A faster/easier inland transport system directly explains shorter warehouse time without requiring another unsupported claim.
  4. B contradicts the stable import quantity. C and D invent information about quality and intention.
Fast method

Treat every inference as 'What must or most likely follows from these facts?'—not 'What story can I imagine?'

Common trap

Extreme words are dangerous unless the passage earns them. 'Always,' 'only,' and claims about motives usually require unusually strong evidence.

Command of Evidence — Textual

Information & Ideas
What it tests

Textual evidence questions ask which piece of information would most directly support or weaken a claim, hypothesis, or conclusion. The task is not to find a merely related statement; it is to identify evidence that changes how credible the specific claim is. You need to match the variables and comparison in the claim to the variables and comparison in the evidence.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Several choices discuss the same topic, but only one addresses the exact claim.
  • The claim predicts a difference between two groups, so useful evidence must compare those groups.
  • A choice may be impressive but irrelevant to the proposed mechanism.
  • The question may ask for support or for evidence that would weaken the claim.
Example problem

A researcher claims that urban trees reduce summer surface temperatures mainly by providing shade rather than by releasing water vapor. Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researcher's claim?

  • A) Tree-lined streets contain more bird species than streets without trees.
  • B) On equally hot days, shaded pavement under artificial canopies is nearly as cool as pavement shaded by trees.
  • C) Trees release more water vapor in summer than in winter.
  • D) Residents report preferring streets with mature trees.
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. The claim compares two possible mechanisms: shade versus water-vapor release.
  2. The strongest evidence should isolate shade from the biological process of transpiration.
  3. Artificial canopies provide shade without releasing water vapor. If they cool pavement almost as much as trees, shade alone explains most of the effect.
  4. C shows that transpiration occurs, but it does not show whether it is the main cause of cooling.
Fast method

Underline the exact claim. Then ask which choice tests the same relationship—not merely the same topic.

Common trap

Relevant is not enough. Evidence must be diagnostic: it should make the claim more or less likely.

Command of Evidence — Quantitative

Information & Ideas
What it tests

Quantitative evidence questions require you to read a table, bar graph, or line graph and use the data to evaluate a statement. The difficulty usually lies in matching the correct rows, columns, units, and comparison—not in advanced arithmetic. The graph is evidence, so the correct answer must be exactly supported by the displayed values.

How the SAT disguises it
  • The passage makes a claim and the graph must confirm it.
  • Two quantities have similar names but different units.
  • A trend is true overall but not for the specific interval named.
  • A choice uses correct numbers but reverses which group had the larger value.
Example problem

A study measured average seedling growth under three light conditions. Full light: 18 cm. Partial shade: 24 cm. Deep shade: 11 cm. Which statement is best supported by the data?

  • A) Seedlings grew fastest as light exposure continually increased.
  • B) Partial shade produced greater average growth than either full light or deep shade.
  • C) Deep shade produced twice the growth observed in full light.
  • D) Full light and partial shade produced identical growth.
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. Translate each condition into its numerical value: full 18, partial 24, deep 11.
  2. Compare the three numbers directly. The largest is 24 under partial shade.
  3. Choice B states exactly that comparison.
  4. A is false because full light is lower than partial shade. C and D conflict with the values.
Fast method

Before reading the answers, summarize the graph in one sentence using the actual numbers or clear comparisons.

Common trap

Do not infer causation or mechanisms from a graph unless the question and study design support them. Quantitative evidence questions often ask only what the displayed data show.

Words in Context

Craft & Structure
What it tests

Words in Context questions test the meaning or rhetorical force of a word or phrase as it functions in a specific sentence. The correct answer is determined by context, tone, and logic—not by the word's most familiar dictionary meaning. Some questions ask you to choose the most precise word to complete a sentence; others ask what an existing word most nearly means in context.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A common word is used in an uncommon academic sense.
  • Several choices are rough synonyms, but only one fits the logic precisely.
  • The blank sits between contrast or cause-and-effect clues that constrain meaning.
  • A word's emotional tone makes one synonym inappropriate even though its dictionary meaning is close.
Example problem

The archaeologist called the newly discovered inscription 'modest' not because it lacked importance but because it consisted of only six short lines carved on an otherwise undecorated stone. As used in the text, 'modest' most nearly means

  • A) humble in behavior
  • B) limited in size or extent
  • C) inexpensive
  • D) uncertain
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. Use the explanation immediately after the word: only six short lines and an undecorated stone.
  2. The sentence explicitly says the inscription does not lack importance, ruling out a meaning like unimportant.
  3. 'Limited in size or extent' matches the physical smallness described.
  4. The behavioral meaning 'humble' does not fit an inscription.
Fast method

Cover the choices and replace the target word with your own simple phrase based on the sentence. Then match that phrase to an option.

Common trap

Do not choose the definition you know best. Context controls meaning.

Text Structure and Purpose

Craft & Structure
What it tests

These questions ask why the author included a sentence, example, quotation, or comparison, or what the passage as a whole is designed to accomplish. You are analyzing the role a piece of text plays inside the larger argument. Common functions include introducing a problem, giving an example, qualifying a claim, presenting evidence, contrasting views, or explaining a mechanism.

How the SAT disguises it
  • The question focuses on one sentence but the answer depends on what comes before and after it.
  • A sentence gives an example; a distractor incorrectly calls it the main conclusion.
  • A passage shifts from an old view to new evidence.
  • The author's purpose is analytical rather than emotional, even if the topic is dramatic.
Example problem

For decades, biologists assumed that a certain desert moss remained dormant whenever rainfall was absent. Recent imaging, however, shows that the moss can briefly activate photosynthesis using moisture from overnight fog. This finding does not overturn the importance of rainfall, but it reveals that the moss has access to an additional water source. What is the function of the second sentence?

  • A) It presents evidence that complicates an earlier assumption.
  • B) It summarizes a debate about whether deserts receive rainfall.
  • C) It gives an example of a species that cannot photosynthesize.
  • D) It argues that rainfall is irrelevant to desert plants.
Answer: A
Deep-dive solution
  1. The first sentence gives the old assumption: no rainfall meant dormancy.
  2. The second introduces new imaging evidence showing activity during fog.
  3. The third sentence explicitly says the new evidence does not erase the importance of rainfall; it adds nuance.
  4. Therefore the second sentence's role is to complicate or revise the earlier assumption.
Fast method

Label each sentence's job in 2–4 words: old view, new evidence, qualification, conclusion. Function questions become much easier.

Common trap

Content and function are different. 'What does this sentence say?' is not the same as 'Why is this sentence here?'

Cross-Text Connections

Craft & Structure
What it tests

Cross-text questions give two short passages on a related topic and ask how their authors' views relate. You may need to identify agreement, disagreement, a likely response, or how one text's evidence affects the other's claim. The core skill is representing each author's position accurately before comparing them.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Both authors agree on the facts but disagree on interpretation.
  • One author is narrower or more cautious rather than directly opposed.
  • The passages use different examples but make compatible claims.
  • A response question requires applying Text 2's principle to a claim from Text 1.
Example problem

Text 1: Researcher Hale argues that remote work generally increases productivity because employees lose less time commuting and can structure work around periods of highest concentration. Text 2: Researcher Imani notes that productivity gains from remote work vary widely by job type; tasks requiring rapid informal collaboration may become slower when coworkers are physically separated. How would Imani most likely respond to Hale's claim?

  • A) By agreeing that remote work always increases productivity
  • B) By arguing that Hale's claim is too broad because productivity effects depend on the kind of work
  • C) By denying that commuting takes time
  • D) By claiming that collaboration never matters for productivity
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. Summarize Hale: remote work generally boosts productivity.
  2. Summarize Imani: effects vary by job type; collaboration-heavy work can be slower remotely.
  3. Imani does not reject every possible benefit of remote work. The objection is to overgeneralization.
  4. Choice B captures that narrower disagreement precisely.
Fast method

Write a five-word summary for each text before comparing them. If you cannot state each position clearly, do not evaluate their relationship yet.

Common trap

Avoid forcing a total disagreement when the second author is simply qualifying the first author's claim.

Rhetorical Synthesis

Expression of Ideas
What it tests

Rhetorical Synthesis questions provide a student's notes and a specific writing goal. Your job is to choose the sentence that uses only relevant information and arranges it to accomplish that goal. The same notes can produce different correct sentences depending on whether the goal is to emphasize a contrast, introduce an example, explain a chronology, define a term, or support a claim.

How the SAT disguises it
  • All choices may be factually true, but only one satisfies the stated goal.
  • A choice includes extra facts that distract from the requested focus.
  • The goal asks for a comparison, but a choice mentions only one item.
  • The notes contain dates, names, and details that must be selectively combined.
Example problem

Notes: • Architect Lina Bo Bardi designed the São Paulo Museum of Art. • The museum's main gallery is suspended above an open public plaza. • The design preserves ground-level space for gatherings and markets. Goal: Emphasize how the building's structure supports public use. Which choice best accomplishes the goal?

  • A) Lina Bo Bardi was an architect who designed a museum in São Paulo.
  • B) The São Paulo Museum of Art includes a main gallery and a public plaza.
  • C) By suspending the main gallery above the ground, Bo Bardi's design preserves an open plaza for public gatherings and markets.
  • D) Markets and public gatherings can occur in many kinds of urban spaces.
Answer: C
Deep-dive solution
  1. Identify the requested relationship: structure → public use.
  2. The key structural fact is the suspended gallery. The key public-use fact is the preserved plaza for gatherings and markets.
  3. Choice C explicitly connects those facts with cause-and-effect wording.
  4. A and B are true but do not fulfill the goal; D is generic and not grounded in the notes.
Fast method

Ignore the notes at first and underline the goal. Then select only the note fragments necessary to achieve that goal.

Common trap

The correct answer is not the sentence with the most facts. It is the sentence with the most goal-relevant facts.

Transitions

Expression of Ideas
What it tests

Transition questions test the logical relationship between ideas. You need to decide whether the new sentence adds, contrasts, gives a result, provides an example, restates, or shifts focus. The best transition expresses that exact relationship and fits grammatically into the sentence.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Several transitions share a broad category but differ in nuance.
  • The relationship spans two sentences, so reading only around the blank is not enough.
  • A sentence looks like a contrast because its topic changes, but logically it is an example or continuation.
  • Punctuation around the transition can eliminate otherwise logical choices.
Example problem

Many early maps of the ocean floor were based on relatively sparse depth measurements. ______, modern sonar systems can collect thousands of depth readings across a wide area in a short time, producing much more detailed maps.

  • A) Similarly,
  • B) In contrast,
  • C) For example,
  • D) Therefore,
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. First sentence: early maps relied on sparse measurements.
  2. Second sentence: modern systems collect many measurements and create detailed maps.
  3. The relationship is a direct contrast between limited older data and abundant modern data.
  4. 'In contrast' expresses that relationship exactly.
Fast method

Name the relationship before considering vocabulary: same direction, contrast, example, cause/result, or clarification.

Common trap

Do not choose a transition because it sounds formal. The logical relationship determines the answer.

Standard English Conventions

Sentence boundaries, grammar, usage, and syntactic clarity.

Boundaries

Standard English
What it tests

Boundaries questions test where sentences and clauses begin and end. You need to distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses and phrases, then choose punctuation that joins them correctly. The core rules include periods, semicolons, commas with coordinating conjunctions, colons after complete clauses, and commas around nonessential elements.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Two independent clauses are incorrectly joined by only a comma.
  • A semicolon is offered where the second unit is not an independent clause.
  • A colon appears after an incomplete clause.
  • A nonessential phrase interrupts the sentence and needs paired punctuation.
Example problem

The telescope's mirror is unusually thin ______ it can therefore adjust its shape slightly as temperature changes.

  • A) ,
  • B) ;
  • C) :
  • D) no punctuation
Answer: B
Deep-dive solution
  1. Before the blank: 'The telescope's mirror is unusually thin' is an independent clause.
  2. After the blank: 'it can therefore adjust its shape slightly as temperature changes' is also an independent clause.
  3. Two independent clauses can be joined by a semicolon without a coordinating conjunction.
  4. A comma alone would create a comma splice. A colon would suggest the second clause explains or lists something introduced by the first, but the cleaner relation here is two closely related independent clauses.
Fast method

For punctuation questions, temporarily ignore the meaning and classify the grammar on each side of the blank: independent clause, dependent clause, or phrase.

Common trap

A semicolon behaves much like a period. If either side cannot stand as a complete sentence, a semicolon is usually wrong.

Form, Structure, and Sense

Standard English
What it tests

This skill covers grammar and usage beyond punctuation: subject-verb agreement, pronouns, verb tense and form, modifiers, possessives, parallelism, comparisons, and sentence structure. The correct choice must fit both grammatical rules and the meaning/time relationships of the sentence.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A long phrase separates the subject from its verb.
  • A plural noun near the verb distracts from a singular subject.
  • Verb tense must match a time clue or another verb.
  • A modifier is placed next to the wrong noun.
  • Items in a list are not grammatically parallel.
Example problem

The collection of meteorite fragments, along with several detailed field notes, ______ stored in a climate-controlled archive.

  • A) are
  • B) were
  • C) is
  • D) have been
Answer: C
Deep-dive solution
  1. Find the grammatical subject: 'collection,' not 'fragments' or 'notes.'
  2. 'Collection' is singular.
  3. The phrase 'of meteorite fragments' and the interrupting phrase 'along with several detailed field notes' do not change the subject's number.
  4. Therefore the singular verb 'is' agrees with the subject.
Fast method

Cross out prepositional phrases and interrupting phrases when checking subject-verb agreement. Then match the true subject to the verb.

Common trap

The noun closest to the verb is often a deliberate distractor. Agreement follows the grammatical subject, not the nearest noun.
