Algebra

Linear equations, functions, systems, and inequalities.

Linear equations in one variable

Algebra
What it tests

This skill tests whether you can create, interpret, rearrange, and solve linear equations that contain a single unknown. The SAT may ask for the value of the variable, the value of an expression such as 3x + 2, or the meaning of a coefficient or constant in context. Harder questions often hide the linear equation inside fractions, parameters, or a word problem rather than presenting it in already-simplified form.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Variables appear on both sides of the equation.
  • A constant such as k must be chosen so the equation has one, no, or infinitely many solutions.
  • A context asks what a coefficient or constant represents.
  • The question asks for an expression in x rather than x itself.
Core idea: preserve equality by performing the same valid operation on both sides. For ax + b = c with a ≠ 0, x = (c − b)/a.
Example problem

For a constant k, the equation 4(x − 3) + k = 2x + 10 has solution x = 7. What is the value of k?

  • A) 4
  • B) 8
  • C) 12
  • D) 16
Answer: B) 8
Deep-dive solution
  1. Substitute the stated solution x = 7. If 7 is a solution, the left and right sides must be equal.
  2. Left side: 4(7 − 3) + k = 16 + k. Right side: 2(7) + 10 = 24.
  3. Set them equal: 16 + k = 24.
  4. Therefore k = 8, so choice B is correct.
Fast method

When a problem gives you a solution and asks for an unknown constant, plug the solution in immediately. This is usually faster than solving the equation symbolically first.

Common trap

Do not automatically divide every term at the beginning. Also watch the wording: the SAT may ask for 2x, x + 4, or a parameter rather than x itself.

Linear functions

Algebra
What it tests

This skill tests whether you understand a linear function as a constant rate of change plus an initial value. You may need to connect a table, graph, equation, or context; determine slope or intercept; interpret units; or compare two linear models. The SAT often cares more about what the slope and intercept mean than about simply calculating them.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A table has equal x-steps and asks for a missing output.
  • A context gives an initial fee plus a per-unit charge.
  • A graph asks for rate of change or an input that produces a target output.
  • Two linear functions are compared by slope, intercept, or intersection.
For f(x) = mx + b, m is the change in output per 1 unit of input, and b = f(0).
Example problem

A water tank contains 860 liters at 9:00 a.m. and 710 liters at 11:30 a.m. The amount of water decreases at a constant rate. At what rate, in liters per hour, is the amount changing?

  • A) −40
  • B) −50
  • C) −60
  • D) −75
Answer: C) −60
Deep-dive solution
  1. The output is liters and the input is time, so slope means liters per hour.
  2. The amount changes by 710 − 860 = −150 liters.
  3. The elapsed time is 2.5 hours, not 2 hours.
  4. Slope = −150 / 2.5 = −60 liters per hour. The negative sign indicates the tank is losing water.
Fast method

Before calculating slope in a word problem, say the units aloud: 'output units per input unit.' That immediately tells you what should be in the numerator and denominator.

Common trap

Do not ignore unequal time intervals. A graph can also show a y-intercept that exists algebraically but has no sensible contextual meaning if x = 0 lies outside the situation.

Linear equations in two variables

Algebra
What it tests

This skill focuses on equations such as Ax + By = C and their graphs. You may solve for one variable, identify slope or intercepts, write an equation from points or a context, or identify parallel and perpendicular lines. The same relationship may be presented in standard form, slope-intercept form, a table, or a graph.

How the SAT disguises it
  • The equation is in standard form and slope is not visually obvious.
  • A point and a slope are given, and you must write the line.
  • A parallel or perpendicular line is requested.
  • The equation models two related quantities and one value is substituted to find the other.
Ax + By = C can be rewritten as y = −(A/B)x + C/B when B ≠ 0. Thus slope = −A/B.
Example problem

Line ℓ is given by 6x + 4y = 20. Which equation represents a line perpendicular to ℓ?

  • A) y = (3/2)x + 4
  • B) y = −(3/2)x + 4
  • C) y = (2/3)x + 4
  • D) y = −(2/3)x + 4
Answer: C) y = (2/3)x + 4
Deep-dive solution
  1. Rewrite or read the slope directly from standard form. For 6x + 4y = 20, slope = −6/4 = −3/2.
  2. A perpendicular nonvertical line has the negative reciprocal slope.
  3. The negative reciprocal of −3/2 is +2/3.
  4. Therefore any line with slope 2/3 is perpendicular; choice C has that slope.
Fast method

For Ax + By = C, memorize slope = −A/B. It saves time when a question is really about parallel or perpendicular lines.

Common trap

Negative reciprocal means both flip the fraction and change the sign. Parallel lines keep the same slope; perpendicular lines do not.

Systems of two linear equations in two variables

Algebra
What it tests

Systems questions test whether you can find values satisfying two linear conditions at once and understand what the number of solutions means graphically. You may solve by substitution or elimination, interpret an intersection in context, or determine parameter values that make two lines intersect once, never, or infinitely many times.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A pricing or mixture problem creates two equations.
  • The question asks for x + y instead of the ordered pair.
  • A parameter controls whether two equations are parallel or identical.
  • A graph is shown and you must identify the system or its solution.
One intersection → one solution. Same slope, different intercepts → no solution. Same line → infinitely many solutions.
Example problem

The system 3x + 2y = 17 and 5x − 2y = 7 has solution (x, y). What is x + y?

  • A) 5
  • B) 6
  • C) 7
  • D) 8
Answer: C) 7
Deep-dive solution
  1. The y-coefficients are opposites, so elimination is immediate.
  2. Add the equations: 8x = 24, giving x = 3.
  3. Substitute into 3x + 2y = 17: 9 + 2y = 17, so y = 4.
  4. The question asks for x + y, not the ordered pair. Therefore x + y = 7. The correct numerical result is 7, so none of the listed options would be valid; in a polished SAT item, one choice must be 7.
Fast method

Scan the coefficients before choosing a method. If a variable already has equal/opposite coefficients, elimination is usually fastest.

Common trap

Always answer the requested quantity. Solving correctly for x and y but entering x when the question asks for x + y is a classic avoidable miss.

Linear inequalities in one or two variables

Algebra
What it tests

This skill tests constraints rather than exact equalities. You may solve a one-variable inequality, translate words such as 'at most' or 'at least,' identify a solution point for a two-variable inequality, or determine the overlap of multiple constraints. Graphical questions require understanding boundary lines and shaded regions.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A budget, capacity, minimum, or maximum creates an inequality.
  • A negative coefficient forces an inequality reversal.
  • A point must be tested in a shaded region.
  • A system of inequalities asks which point satisfies every condition.
Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative reverses the inequality sign. For a two-variable inequality, replace the sign with equality to get the boundary line.
Example problem

A theater can seat at most 420 people. Adult tickets are represented by a and student tickets by s. At least 120 tickets sold must be adult tickets. Which system represents the constraints?

  • A) a + s ≥ 420 and a ≥ 120
  • B) a + s ≤ 420 and a ≥ 120
  • C) a + s ≤ 420 and a ≤ 120
  • D) a + s = 420 and a ≥ 120
Answer: B) a + s ≤ 420 and a ≥ 120
Deep-dive solution
  1. 'At most 420' means the total cannot exceed 420, so a + s ≤ 420.
  2. 'At least 120 adult tickets' means a cannot be below 120, so a ≥ 120.
  3. The seats do not have to be completely filled, so equality alone is too restrictive.
  4. Combine both conditions: a + s ≤ 420 and a ≥ 120.
Fast method

Translate phrases mechanically: at most → ≤, no more than → ≤, at least → ≥, no fewer than → ≥.

Common trap

A solid graph boundary corresponds to ≤ or ≥; a dashed boundary corresponds to < or >. And never forget to reverse the sign after multiplying or dividing by a negative.

Advanced Math

Equivalent expressions, nonlinear equations, and nonlinear functions.

Equivalent expressions

Advanced Math
What it tests

Equivalent-expression questions test whether you can rewrite algebra without changing its value. This includes factoring, expanding, combining rational expressions, exponent and radical rules, and recognizing algebraic structure. The hardest versions ask which form reveals a specific feature such as a zero, coefficient, growth factor, or constant.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A polynomial must be factored to expose roots.
  • An expression contains rational or negative exponents.
  • A rational expression has a hidden common factor.
  • An identity contains an unknown coefficient that can be found by matching terms.
Useful structures include a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b), (a ± b)² = a² ± 2ab + b², and a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(a^m).
Example problem

Which expression is equivalent to 9x² − 24x + 16?

  • A) (3x − 4)²
  • B) (9x − 4)²
  • C) (3x + 4)²
  • D) (9x − 16)(x − 1)
Answer: A) (3x − 4)²
Deep-dive solution
  1. Recognize the first and last terms as squares: 9x² = (3x)² and 16 = 4².
  2. A perfect-square trinomial with a negative middle term has form a² − 2ab + b².
  3. Here −2(3x)(4) = −24x, exactly matching the middle term.
  4. Therefore 9x² − 24x + 16 = (3x − 4)².
Fast method

Before expanding anything, inspect the first term, last term, and middle coefficient for common identities. Structural recognition is much faster than brute force.

Common trap

You may cancel factors in a rational expression, not terms separated by addition or subtraction. Also remember √(x²) = |x|, not simply x for every real x.

Nonlinear equations in one variable and systems in two variables

Advanced Math
What it tests

This skill includes quadratic, polynomial, absolute-value, rational, and radical equations, plus systems containing at least one nonlinear equation. You may solve explicitly, determine the number of real solutions, find a parameter that changes the number of solutions, or match algebraic solutions to graph intersections.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A quadratic must be factored, completed to a square, or solved with the quadratic formula.
  • The discriminant controls how many real roots exist.
  • Squaring a radical equation can create an extraneous solution.
  • A line and parabola are solved together by substitution.
For ax² + bx + c = 0, x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)]/(2a). The discriminant D = b² − 4ac controls the number of real roots.
Example problem

For what value of k does x² − 6x + k = 0 have exactly one real solution?

  • A) 6
  • B) 8
  • C) 9
  • D) 12
Answer: C) 9
Deep-dive solution
  1. Exactly one real solution means the quadratic has a repeated root.
  2. That happens when the discriminant equals zero.
  3. D = (−6)² − 4(1)(k) = 36 − 4k.
  4. Set 36 − 4k = 0, giving k = 9.
Fast method

If the question asks for the number of quadratic solutions or a parameter that changes that number, think discriminant before solving the roots themselves.

Common trap

After squaring a radical equation or clearing rational denominators, check candidates in the original equation. Algebraic transformations can introduce values that were never allowed.

Nonlinear functions

Advanced Math
What it tests

This skill tests quadratic, exponential, polynomial, rational, and other nonlinear functions as relationships rather than isolated equations. You may interpret constants, compare models, identify a maximum or minimum, transform a graph, connect equations to tables, or choose a form that reveals a desired feature.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Vertex form reveals a quadratic maximum/minimum.
  • Factored form reveals zeros.
  • An exponential growth factor must be converted to a percent rate.
  • A graph or table must be identified as linear, quadratic, or exponential.
  • A transformed function shifts, reflects, or stretches a parent graph.
Quadratic vertex form: f(x) = a(x − h)² + k. Exponential form: f(x) = ab^x, where a = f(0) and b is the factor for each +1 change in x.
Example problem

A population is modeled by P(t) = 480(1.06)^t, where t is years after 2026. Which statement is true?

  • A) The population starts at 480 and increases by 6% each year.
  • B) The population starts at 480 and increases by 106% each year.
  • C) The population starts at 1.06 and increases by 480 each year.
  • D) The population starts at 480 and increases by 0.06 people each year.
Answer: A) The population starts at 480 and increases by 6% each year.
Deep-dive solution
  1. At t = 0, P(0) = 480(1.06)^0 = 480, so 480 is the initial value.
  2. The base 1.06 is the multiplicative factor per year.
  3. A factor of 1.06 means retain 100% and add 6%, so the annual growth rate is 6%.
  4. The model is exponential because the same percentage, not the same number, is added each year.
Fast method

For ab^x, ask two questions immediately: what is a, and what does b mean? b = 1 + r for growth and b = 1 − r for decay.

Common trap

Do not confuse a 1.06 multiplier with 106% growth. It represents 106% of the previous value, which is a 6% increase.

Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

Rates, percentages, data, probability, inference, and study design.

Ratios, rates, proportional relationships, and units

PSDA
What it tests

This skill measures quantitative reasoning with ratios, unit rates, direct proportionality, dimensional analysis, scale drawings, speed, density, and derived units. The SAT often embeds the arithmetic in a real-world or science context, so correct units and the reference quantity matter as much as the computation.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A ratio must be converted into a part of a whole.
  • A rate uses compound units such as miles per hour or grams per cubic centimeter.
  • A scale factor changes length and possibly area or volume.
  • Multiple unit conversions are required before solving.
Unit rate = output quantity / input quantity. In direct proportion, y = kx and k = y/x.
Example problem

A map uses a scale of 1.5 centimeters to represent 24 kilometers. Two cities are 5.25 centimeters apart on the map. What is the actual distance?

  • A) 56 km
  • B) 72 km
  • C) 84 km
  • D) 96 km
Answer: C) 84 km
Deep-dive solution
  1. Find the distance represented by 1 centimeter: 24 / 1.5 = 16 kilometers per centimeter.
  2. Multiply the map distance by the unit rate: 5.25 × 16.
  3. 5 × 16 = 80 and 0.25 × 16 = 4, so the actual distance is 84 kilometers.
  4. The units cancel conceptually: cm × (km/cm) = km.
Fast method

Write conversion factors with units and let the units cancel. If the units do not cancel to the requested unit, the setup is wrong.

Common trap

For area and volume, conversion factors must be squared or cubed. If 1 m = 100 cm, then 1 m² = 10,000 cm², not 100 cm².

Percentages

PSDA
What it tests

Percentage questions test percent of a quantity, percent increase/decrease, growth factors, discounts, taxes, tips, simple interest, and reverse-percent reasoning. Harder questions often use successive percentage changes or ask you to recover an original value from a final value.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A final price includes tax or discount.
  • A quantity changes by multiple percentages in sequence.
  • The original amount is unknown after a stated percent change.
  • Percent change must be distinguished from percentage-point change.
New = Original(1 ± r), with r written as a decimal. Percent change = (New − Original)/Original × 100%.
Example problem

After a 20% discount and then an 8% sales tax, a jacket costs $86.40. What was the original price?

  • A) $90
  • B) $96
  • C) $100
  • D) $108
Answer: C) $100
Deep-dive solution
  1. A 20% discount multiplies the original by 0.80.
  2. An 8% tax then multiplies the discounted price by 1.08.
  3. Final = Original × 0.80 × 1.08 = Original × 0.864.
  4. 86.40 = 0.864 × Original, so Original = 100.
Fast method

Convert each percentage change to a multiplier and multiply the factors in order. This is cleaner than repeatedly finding percentage amounts.

Common trap

Equal percentage increase and decrease do not cancel because the second percent is taken from a different base. +20% then −20% gives 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96.

One-variable data: distributions and measures of center and spread

PSDA
What it tests

This skill tests how a single quantitative variable is distributed. You may interpret tables, dot plots, histograms, and box plots; compare mean and median; calculate range or IQR; reason about standard deviation; identify outliers; or predict how adding, removing, or shifting values changes summary statistics.

How the SAT disguises it
  • An outlier changes mean more than median.
  • Two distributions have the same center but different spread.
  • A constant is added to every data value.
  • A box plot must be compared without knowing every raw value.
Mean = sum/n. Range = max − min. IQR = Q3 − Q1. Standard deviation describes typical spread around the mean.
Example problem

A data set has mean 18. If every value in the data set is increased by 5, what are the new mean and the new standard deviation?

  • A) Mean 18; standard deviation increases by 5
  • B) Mean 23; standard deviation unchanged
  • C) Mean 23; standard deviation increases by 5
  • D) Mean 90; standard deviation unchanged
Answer: B) Mean 23; standard deviation unchanged
Deep-dive solution
  1. Adding 5 to every observation shifts the entire distribution 5 units to the right.
  2. Because every value rises by 5, the mean rises by 5: 18 → 23.
  3. Distances between the data values do not change, and each value remains the same distance from the new mean as before.
  4. Therefore the standard deviation, a measure of spread, stays unchanged.
Fast method

Adding a constant shifts center but not spread. Multiplying every value by a constant multiplies both center and spread by the magnitude of that constant.

Common trap

A box plot reveals quartiles and range, not the exact mean. Do not invent information the graph does not encode.

Two-variable data: models and scatterplots

PSDA
What it tests

This skill tests relationships between two variables. You may interpret direction and strength of association, choose a linear/quadratic/exponential model, use a model for prediction, interpret slope or other parameters, or calculate and interpret residuals. The SAT may also ask whether extrapolation is sensible.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A scatterplot suggests a model type.
  • A line of best fit is used to make a prediction.
  • An observed point is compared with a predicted point.
  • The wording tries to turn association into causation.
For a linear model ŷ = mx + b, residual = observed y − predicted ŷ.
Example problem

A model predicts that a plant will be 42 cm tall after 6 weeks. Its actual height after 6 weeks is 47 cm. What is the residual?

  • A) −5
  • B) 5
  • C) 42
  • D) 89
Answer: B) 5
Deep-dive solution
  1. Residual is observed minus predicted, not the other way around.
  2. Observed height = 47; predicted height = 42.
  3. Residual = 47 − 42 = 5.
  4. A positive residual means the observed point lies above the model's prediction.
Fast method

Memorize 'residual = real minus model.' Positive → point above the fitted model; negative → point below.

Common trap

Correlation or a good-fitting model does not by itself establish causation. Also be cautious about predictions far beyond the x-values actually observed.

Probability and conditional probability

PSDA
What it tests

Probability questions test favorable outcomes, complements, unions, intersections, conditional probability, and independence. They are commonly presented with two-way tables, area models, or short scenarios. The key challenge is choosing the correct denominator—the entire sample space or only the conditioned group.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A two-way table asks for a joint or conditional proportion.
  • 'At least one' is easier through a complement.
  • Two events may overlap, requiring subtraction of the intersection.
  • Independence must be distinguished from mutual exclusivity.
P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B). General addition: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
Example problem

Of 80 students, 50 study French, 32 study music, and 20 study both. If a student who studies French is selected at random, what is the probability that the student also studies music?

  • A) 1/4
  • B) 2/5
  • C) 5/8
  • D) 13/20
Answer: B) 2/5
Deep-dive solution
  1. The condition 'who studies French' restricts the sample space to the 50 French students.
  2. Among those 50, 20 also study music.
  3. So P(music | French) = 20/50 = 2/5.
  4. The total 80 students is irrelevant once the condition has restricted the denominator.
Fast method

Circle or underline the condition after the word 'given' or the phrase 'among those who.' That group becomes your denominator.

Common trap

Mutually exclusive events with nonzero probability are not independent: if one occurs, the other becomes impossible.

Inference from sample statistics and margin of error

PSDA
What it tests

This skill tests how sample means and proportions estimate population parameters. You may identify the population a sample can represent, use a sample proportion to estimate a count, interpret a margin of error, or reason about how sample size affects precision. The SAT focuses on conceptual interpretation rather than formal confidence-interval computation.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A survey result is extended to a larger population.
  • An estimate is reported with ± a margin of error.
  • Two polls with different sample sizes are compared.
  • A biased sample is contrasted with a larger but still biased sample.
Estimate interval = sample estimate ± margin of error. Larger comparable random samples generally produce smaller margins of error.
Example problem

A random sample of 600 city residents finds that 54% support a proposal, with a margin of error of 4 percentage points. Which population percentage is consistent with the estimate?

  • A) 47%
  • B) 49%
  • C) 52%
  • D) 60%
Answer: C) 52%
Deep-dive solution
  1. Form the interval: 54% − 4% = 50% and 54% + 4% = 58%.
  2. Values from 50% through 58% are consistent with the reported estimate.
  3. 52% lies inside that interval.
  4. 49%, 47%, and 60% lie outside it.
Fast method

When given estimate ± margin of error, immediately write the lower and upper endpoints before looking at the choices.

Common trap

A larger sample reduces random sampling variability; it does not repair a biased sampling method. Bias and margin of error are different problems.

Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments

PSDA
What it tests

This skill tests study design and the strength of conclusions. You need to distinguish random sampling from random assignment, observational studies from experiments, association from causation, and the population sampled from the population a claim tries to describe. This is largely a reasoning skill, not a calculation skill.

How the SAT disguises it
  • Participants self-select into a study.
  • Treatments are randomly assigned but the participants were not randomly sampled.
  • A random sample is observed without manipulating a variable.
  • A study finds an association and the conclusion incorrectly claims causation.
Random sampling supports generalization to the sampled population. Random assignment in a well-designed experiment supports causal conclusions.
Example problem

Researchers randomly assign 200 volunteers to either drink caffeinated tea or caffeine-free tea before a memory task. The caffeinated group performs better on average. Which conclusion is best supported?

  • A) Caffeine caused better memory-task performance for the volunteers in this experiment.
  • B) Caffeine improves memory for all adults.
  • C) People with better memories are more likely to choose caffeine.
  • D) The study proves tea improves long-term academic achievement.
Answer: A) Caffeine caused better memory-task performance for the volunteers in this experiment.
Deep-dive solution
  1. The researchers manipulated the treatment: caffeinated versus caffeine-free tea.
  2. They randomly assigned volunteers to the treatment groups, which supports a causal comparison within the study.
  3. However, the participants were volunteers rather than a random sample of all adults.
  4. Therefore the strongest justified conclusion is causal for these participants or similar participants, not automatically for every adult.
Fast method

Ask two separate questions: Was there random sampling? Was there random assignment? They justify different kinds of conclusions.

Common trap

Do not treat 'random' as one magic word. Random sample → generalization. Random assignment → causation.

Geometry and Trigonometry

Area and volume, angle relationships, right triangles, trigonometry, and circles.

Area and volume

Geometry & Trig
What it tests

This skill includes perimeter, area, surface area, volume, composite figures, and scale factors. You may need to choose the correct formula, derive a missing dimension first, or reason how changing every linear dimension changes area or volume. Contextual geometry often tests units as much as formulas.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A composite figure must be split or subtracted.
  • A solid's base area is given rather than its dimensions.
  • A scale factor changes area by k² or volume by k³.
  • A radius, diameter, height, or slant height must be identified correctly.
Typical structures: triangle A = ½bh; circle A = πr²; prism V = Bh; cylinder V = πr²h; cone/pyramid V = ⅓Bh; sphere V = 4πr³/3.
Example problem

A cylindrical container has radius 4 cm and height 9 cm. A second similar container has every linear dimension 1.5 times as large. How many times the volume of the first container is the volume of the second?

  • A) 1.5
  • B) 2.25
  • C) 3.375
  • D) 4.5
Answer: C) 3.375
Deep-dive solution
  1. The containers are similar, and every length is multiplied by k = 1.5.
  2. Volume scales with the cube of the linear scale factor.
  3. Volume factor = 1.5³ = 3.375.
  4. No cylinder formula calculation is actually needed; scale reasoning is faster.
Fast method

For similar figures, length → k, area → k², volume → k³. Write that trio at the top of your scratch work.

Common trap

Use perpendicular height for area and volume formulas. A slanted edge is not automatically the height.

Lines, angles, and triangles

Geometry & Trig
What it tests

This skill tests angle relationships, parallel lines cut by a transversal, triangle angle sums, exterior angles, congruence, similarity, and scale factor. Many questions are less about computation and more about recognizing which theorem makes a missing angle or side determined.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A diagram is not drawn to scale.
  • Parallel-line marks make corresponding or alternate-interior angles equal.
  • Two equal angles establish AA similarity.
  • A side proportion depends on matching corresponding vertices correctly.
Triangle interior angles sum to 180°. Vertical angles are equal. If lines are parallel, corresponding and alternate-interior angles are equal; same-side interior angles sum to 180°.
Example problem

Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One acute angle formed is 68°. What is the measure of each obtuse angle formed by the transversal?

  • A) 22°
  • B) 68°
  • C) 112°
  • D) 136°
Answer: C) 112°
Deep-dive solution
  1. With parallel lines, all acute angles formed by the transversal are congruent, so each acute angle is 68°.
  2. Each adjacent acute/obtuse pair forms a straight angle of 180°.
  3. Obtuse angle = 180° − 68° = 112°.
  4. Vertical and corresponding angle relationships then repeat that same obtuse measure at the other positions.
Fast method

In a standard parallel-lines/transversal diagram, once you know one non-right angle θ, every angle is either θ or 180° − θ.

Common trap

Do not trust the visual size of an angle. Only marks, given measures, and theorems justify equality.

Right triangles and trigonometry

Geometry & Trig
What it tests

This skill includes the Pythagorean theorem, 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles, sine/cosine/tangent, similar right triangles, and complementary-angle relationships. The SAT often gives enough information for a shortcut; the main challenge is labeling sides relative to the correct angle.

How the SAT disguises it
  • A missing side can be found by a special-triangle ratio instead of Pythagorean theorem.
  • The reference angle changes which leg is opposite or adjacent.
  • A trig ratio is inferred from similar triangles.
  • sin θ is connected to cos(90° − θ).
a² + b² = c². sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse; cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse; tan θ = opposite/adjacent.
Example problem

In a right triangle, sin θ = 5/13 for an acute angle θ. What is cos θ?

  • A) 5/12
  • B) 12/13
  • C) 13/12
  • D) 8/13
Answer: B) 12/13
Deep-dive solution
  1. sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse = 5/13, so model the opposite side as 5 and hypotenuse as 13.
  2. Use the Pythagorean theorem for the adjacent side: adjacent² + 5² = 13².
  3. adjacent² = 169 − 25 = 144, so adjacent = 12.
  4. cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse = 12/13.
Fast method

If a trig ratio resembles a familiar Pythagorean triple—3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17—use it immediately.

Common trap

Opposite and adjacent depend on which acute angle is named. The hypotenuse never changes: it is opposite the 90° angle.

Circles

Geometry & Trig
What it tests

Circle questions can test circumference and area, central and inscribed angles, tangents, arcs, sectors, radians, coordinate-circle equations, distance, the unit circle, or completing the square. The SAT may combine algebra and geometry in the same item, so recognizing the geometric meaning of an equation is important.

How the SAT disguises it
  • An equation must be completed to a square to reveal center and radius.
  • An inscribed angle is half its intercepted arc.
  • Arc length or sector area is a fraction of a whole circle.
  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency.
  • Degrees must be converted to radians.
(x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Arc length = (θ/360°)(2πr) in degrees or rθ in radians. Sector area = (θ/360°)πr².
Example problem

The circle x² + y² − 8x + 6y = 0 has center (h, k) and radius r. What is r?

  • A) 3
  • B) 4
  • C) 5
  • D) 7
Answer: C) 5
Deep-dive solution
  1. Group x-terms and y-terms: x² − 8x + y² + 6y = 0.
  2. Complete the squares: x² − 8x + 16 = (x − 4)² and y² + 6y + 9 = (y + 3)².
  3. Add 16 and 9 to both sides: (x − 4)² + (y + 3)² = 25.
  4. The right side is r², so r = 5.
Fast method

For x² + bx, add (b/2)² to complete the square. Keep a running total of everything added so you can add the same amount to the other side.

Common trap

In circle form, the signs inside parentheses are opposite the center coordinates, and the right side is r²—not r.
